# Module 06 assessment: PNT Threats and Vulnerabilities

## match
Match each concept to the correct PNT example.
- Threat / adverse condition = Space weather disturbing the ionosphere
- Vulnerability = A single antenna shared by both receivers
- PNT harm = Timing reference biased and integrity lost
- Mission consequence = Autonomy restricted and synchronisation lost
> Separating cause from effect keeps threat modelling mission-led rather than a list of hostile techniques.

## match
Match each failure state to what the user experiences.
- Denied / unavailable = Required PNT information cannot be obtained
- Degraded = PNT remains available but no longer meets required performance
- Deceived / misleading = PNT appears usable but is materially wrong
> Classifying the effect on the user keeps the analysis focused on operational consequence.

## single
Why is deception often a more serious integrity hazard than denial?
- Because deception is always more expensive for the attacker.
* Because a clean loss can trigger a fallback, whereas convincing but wrong PNT may keep driving automation and human decisions.
- Because denial cannot affect timing systems.
- Because deceived receivers always shut down.
> Misleading information can propagate unnoticed. That makes it a particularly important integrity hazard.

## single
Why are GNSS signals particularly susceptible to radio-frequency interference?
- Because they are transmitted at very high power.
* Because they travel thousands of kilometres and arrive at very low received power in a noisy RF environment.
- Because GNSS receivers do not use antennas.
- Because GNSS uses only one frequency worldwide.
> Weak received signals make GNSS useful but mean local RF energy can interfere with reception.

## truefalse
Every GNSS interference event should be described as jamming.
= false
> Interference is unwanted RF energy; jamming is deliberate interference. Report what the evidence supports, for example "GNSS interference was observed".

## multi
Which observations could indicate jamming or a common RF problem?
* Carrier-to-noise falling across several unrelated satellites at once
* Multiple nearby systems losing GNSS at a similar time
* An INS continuing normally while GNSS measurements disappear
- One satellite setting below the horizon
> Losing one satellite can be normal. Many signals degrading together suggests a common RF problem; detection relies on patterns and independent evidence.

## single
What distinguishes spoofing from jamming?
- Spoofing only affects timing; jamming only affects position.
* Spoofing aims to make a receiver compute a false but plausible solution, making it an integrity problem; jamming aims to deny or degrade reception.
- Spoofing is always accidental.
- There is no difference; they are synonyms.
> Spoofing is deception rather than obvious loss of service, and a spoofed solution may initially appear plausible.

## single
What does meaconing demonstrate about authentication?
- Authenticated signals can never be misleading.
* Authentic source data does not prove the signal arrived through the expected path, at the expected time, without delay or rebroadcast.
- Meaconing only works on unauthenticated military signals.
- Authentication prevents all forms of deception.
> Meaconing rebroadcasts genuine signals, often with delay or from another location, so the propagation context is altered.

## single
A correction service is compromised and delivers subtly biased corrections. Which statement is correct?
- This cannot affect PNT because the satellite signals are untouched.
* It is a data-layer threat that can bias a high-accuracy solution without any attack on the RF signal.
- Only the RF front end needs protecting.
- It will always cause total loss of service.
> PNT assurance is cyber-physical. Software, data, networks and interfaces are attack and failure surfaces too.

## single
A design uses two GNSS receivers for redundancy. Which finding would reveal a hidden single point of failure?
- The receivers are from different manufacturers.
* Both receivers share the same antenna and power supply.
- The receivers are mounted a metre apart.
- One receiver is newer than the other.
> A design that looks redundant on a block diagram can hide shared antennas, power, software, correction providers or network paths.

## single
A supplier says their timing service is "independent" of your GNSS receiver. What should you establish?
- Only whether its interface looks different.
* What it depends on upstream, because independence must survive the failure being analysed; for example, it may itself be disciplined by GNSS.
- Whether it is cheaper than GNSS.
- Whether it uses the same connector type.
> Independence has to hold under the real-world failure, not merely look different at the interface.

## order
Put the stages of the PNT risk lifecycle in order.
1. Govern
2. Identify
3. Protect
4. Detect
5. Respond
6. Recover
> The lifecycle, aligned with NIST CSF 2.0, connects governance and dependency identification with protection, detection, response and recovery.

## single
GNSS returns after a suspected spoofing incident. What should the system do?
- Immediately trust the first returning signal to restore full service.
* Revalidate sources against independent information and restore trust under defined criteria before resuming full reliance.
- Permanently disable GNSS.
- Reset the system to factory defaults.
> Recovery is not simply "GNSS is back". Trusting the first returning signal can preserve a corrupted solution.

## single
A vessel's GNSS position diverges from its route while the receiver reports a valid fix. What is the correct first priority?
- Determine whether the cause is spoofing before doing anything else.
* Compare GNSS with independent sources and, if they disagree beyond expected uncertainty, treat GNSS as suspect and respond safely; attribution can come later.
- Trust the receiver because it reports a valid fix.
- Switch off all navigation systems.
> Detection of misleading PNT is about inconsistency with independent truth or physics. Attribution is secondary to a timely safe response.

## single
Which statement about graceful degradation is correct?
- A system is either fully operational or failed; intermediate states add risk.
* Defined intermediate states (degraded, restricted, safe state, recovery) let the system retain useful capability while reducing function or confidence explicitly.
- Graceful degradation means hiding degraded performance from the user.
- It only applies to timing systems.
> "Resilient to GNSS loss" is not measurable until duration, performance and behaviour in each state are specified.
